# Academic Research Writing: End-to-End Workflow and Working Manual

**Purpose:** A practical, discipline-neutral standard operating procedure (SOP) for essays, research papers, proposals, literature reviews, theses/dissertations, and journal manuscripts.  
**Style baseline:** APA 7th edition, unless the assignment, institution, supervisor, or target journal says otherwise.  
**Prepared:** 12 August 2026

---

## 1. What academic research writing entails

Academic research writing is not simply “finding articles and rewriting them.” It is a documented process of:

1. interpreting a brief or identifying a defensible research problem;
2. forming a focused question, objectives, and—when appropriate—hypotheses;
3. searching for relevant evidence systematically;
4. checking source quality and reading the sources critically;
5. organizing evidence into themes, debates, methods, and gaps;
6. selecting and applying an appropriate research design;
7. analyzing data or synthesizing published evidence;
8. making a clear argument supported by verifiable sources;
9. citing every borrowed idea, fact, quotation, table, figure, or dataset;
10. revising for logic, evidence, structure, language, formatting, and integrity; and
11. submitting the work with the required supporting files.

### The central quality rule

A strong paper aligns the following elements:

> **Problem → question → objective → evidence/method → analysis → conclusion**

If the conclusion does not answer the research question, or the method cannot produce the evidence needed to answer it, the project is misaligned.

### Ethical boundary for a professional academic writer

Legitimate services include research assistance, source discovery, coaching, editing, formatting, data-analysis support, and collaborative drafting with genuine authors. Do not fabricate data or citations, impersonate a researcher, conceal conflicts, or produce work that a student will falsely submit as entirely their own where institutional rules prohibit that practice. Authorship requires real intellectual contribution and accountability.

## 2. The complete research flow

```text
1. Brief/intake
      ↓
2. Topic and feasibility check
      ↓
3. Problem statement, question, objectives, hypotheses
      ↓
4. Review type or research design selected
      ↓
5. Search protocol and search terms prepared
      ↓
6. Literature searched, exported, deduplicated, retrieved
      ↓
7. Sources screened and quality-appraised
      ↓
8. Evidence matrix, themes, framework, and gap developed
      ↓
9A. Literature-only study: evidence synthesis
                   OR
9B. Empirical study: ethics → sampling → data collection → analysis
      ↓
10. Detailed outline and argument map
      ↓
11. Draft section by section with citations
      ↓
12. Content, method, citation, APA, and language audits
      ↓
13. Supervisor/coauthor feedback and revision
      ↓
14. Final submission package and archive
```

## 3. Stage-by-stage SOP

| Stage | Essential manual work | Helpful tools/sites | Output or “done” condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Intake** | Read the brief; identify audience, purpose, length, deadline, required source type/date range, style, deliverables, and assessment rubric. | Assignment brief, journal author guidelines, institutional handbook | One-page project specification; ambiguities resolved before research begins |
| **2. Scope and feasibility** | Narrow population, place, variables/concepts, period, and context. Check whether enough credible evidence or accessible data exists. | Google Scholar, library discovery service, Scopus/Web of Science if available | Feasible working topic with boundaries and exclusions |
| **3. Question and objectives** | Write the problem, main question, subquestions, objectives, and hypotheses where applicable. Define key terms operationally. | PICO/PICOT, SPIDER, PCC, FINER frameworks | Each objective directly supports the main question |
| **4. Plan/protocol** | Choose paper/review type or methodology. Predefine databases, eligibility criteria, quality checks, analysis plan, and milestones. | OSF Registries; PROSPERO for eligible health reviews; PRISMA for systematic reviews | Written protocol or research plan; no silent method changes later |
| **5. Search design** | Identify concepts, synonyms, controlled vocabulary, Boolean operators, limits, and citation-chasing strategy. Pilot and revise the search. | Database thesauri; MeSH in PubMed; ERIC descriptors; PsycINFO subject headings | Reproducible search strings and search log |
| **6. Search and retrieval** | Run searches; export complete records; remove duplicates; locate lawful full text; record dates and result counts. | Zotero, EndNote, Mendeley; Unpaywall; CORE; institutional library/ILL | Search library with PDFs/links, complete metadata, and no obvious duplicates |
| **7. Screening** | Apply title/abstract and then full-text inclusion rules consistently. Record exclusion reasons. | Rayyan or spreadsheet; PRISMA flow diagram when applicable | Final included set plus auditable exclusion record |
| **8. Critical appraisal** | Assess relevance, design, sample, measures, analysis, bias, limitations, conflicts, and publication status. | CASP, JBI, MMAT, RoB 2, ROBINS-I, AACODS—select the tool appropriate to the study type | Quality notes or ratings for every central source |
| **9. Evidence extraction** | Read the actual document; capture findings, page numbers, definitions, quotations, methods, limitations, and your interpretation. | Literature/evidence matrix; PDF annotation | Every planned claim is traceable to a source and page/section |
| **10. Synthesis/framework** | Compare rather than list studies. Group by themes, theories, methods, populations, agreement, disagreement, strength, and gaps. | Concept map; synthesis table; argument map | Thematic structure and defensible research gap |
| **11. Method/analysis** | For empirical work: design sampling, instruments, ethics, data management, and analysis. For reviews: apply the protocol and synthesize included evidence. | R, SPSS, Stata, Python, NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA, Excel—as appropriate | Transparent method and results that can be checked or reproduced |
| **12. Outline** | Assign one purpose to each section and one claim to each paragraph. Link evidence to objectives. | Hierarchical outline; reverse outline | Full argument visible before prose drafting |
| **13. Draft** | Write original analysis; integrate evidence; distinguish findings from interpretation; cite while writing. | Word/Google Docs/LaTeX; citation-manager plugin | Complete draft with no unsupported placeholders |
| **14. Revise and audit** | Perform separate audits for argument, evidence, methods, citations, APA, language, tables/figures, and requirements. | Spell/grammar checker; Crossref; reference manager; plagiarism/similarity report interpreted by a human | Every quality checklist passes |
| **15. Submit/archive** | Prepare title page, manuscript, references, appendices, tables/figures, data/code statements, cover letter, and required declarations. Save source files and final version. | Journal portal or institutional repository; ORCID; OSF/Zenodo where appropriate | Correct files submitted; confirmation and reproducible archive retained |

## 4. The manual tasks that cannot be delegated blindly to software

The following require human academic judgment:

- deciding whether the research question matters and is answerable;
- deciding whether a paper is actually relevant, not merely keyword-similar;
- reading the full source and checking that it supports the proposed claim;
- distinguishing correlation, causation, association, prediction, and opinion;
- assessing methods, risk of bias, conflicts of interest, and limitations;
- deciding how much weight each source deserves;
- reconciling contradictory findings;
- developing the argument, interpretation, implications, and limitations;
- ensuring methods are appropriate and statistics are interpreted correctly;
- verifying every citation, quotation, DOI, table, figure, and numerical value;
- protecting participants and confidential data; and
- approving the final text and accepting responsibility for it.

### Appropriate uses of software

Software can speed up discovery, metadata import, deduplication, coding, calculation, formatting, grammar checking, version control, and similarity checking. It cannot establish truth merely by generating polished language. A citation produced by any automated system must be opened and verified against the real source.

## 5. Where to source credible documents

### A. Start with the researcher’s library

Use the institution’s library discovery system, subject librarian, database list, proxy/VPN login, and interlibrary loan (ILL). This is often the best legal route to paywalled journal articles, books, theses, and specialist databases.

### B. Broad discovery and citation mapping

| Site | Best use | Access and caution |
|---|---|---|
| [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/) | Broad topic exploration; “cited by”; related works; author profiles | Free. Coverage is broad but quality is mixed; do not treat ranking as a quality verdict. |
| [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/) | Discovery, references, citations, and related-paper mapping | Free. Verify generated summaries and the original text. |
| [OpenAlex](https://openalex.org/) | Open scholarly metadata, works, authors, institutions, and citation relationships | Free/open. A metadata record is not necessarily full text or peer reviewed. |
| [Crossref Metadata Search](https://search.crossref.org/) | Verify title, authors, journal details, and DOI | Free. Best for metadata verification, not critical appraisal. |
| [Scopus](https://www.scopus.com/) | Multidisciplinary abstract/citation searching and citation analysis | Subscription, usually through a library. |
| [Web of Science](https://www.webofscience.com/) | Curated citation indexes and backward/forward citation searching | Subscription, usually through a library. |

### C. Discipline-specific databases

| Discipline | Recommended starting points |
|---|---|
| Medicine, nursing, public health | [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/), Embase and CINAHL through libraries |
| Psychology and behavioral science | APA PsycINFO through a library; PubMed; Scopus/Web of Science |
| Education | [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/), Education Source through a library |
| Engineering, computing, electronics | [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/), [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/) for preprints |
| Economics and business | EconLit through a library; [RePEc](https://repec.org/), [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/), Scopus/Web of Science |
| Humanities and social sciences | [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/), Project MUSE, Scopus/Web of Science, subject databases |
| Law | HeinOnline, Westlaw, Lexis, official court and government sites; [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/) for working papers |
| Agriculture and food | [AGRICOLA](https://agricola.nal.usda.gov/), CAB Abstracts through a library, FAO resources |
| Multidisciplinary theses | ProQuest Dissertations & Theses through a library; institutional repositories; [OATD](https://oatd.org/) |

**Important:** Databases index different material. An indexed record is not automatically peer reviewed, correct, relevant, or accessible in full text. Verify the document type and publication status.

### D. Legal open-access and full-text sources

- [Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/) — journals and articles in open-access publications.
- [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/) — content aggregated from repositories.
- [BASE](https://www.base-search.net/) — academic repository search.
- [Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/) — lawful open-access locations for articles.
- [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/) — biomedical/life-science full text and records.
- [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/), [OSF](https://osf.io/), and institutional repositories — papers, preprints, protocols, data, and software.
- [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/), [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/), [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/), and [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/) — preprints or working papers. Label them accurately and check whether a peer-reviewed version exists.

For paywalled work, use library access, ILL/document delivery, a lawful repository copy, or contact the author. Do not use pirated copies.

### E. Government, policy, statistics, and datasets

Prefer the issuing organization and the original dataset/report:

- national statistics and official `.gov` sites, such as [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/) and [Data.gov](https://data.gov/);
- [World Bank Data](https://data.worldbank.org/), [UN Data](https://data.un.org/), [WHO](https://www.who.int/data), [OECD Data](https://data.oecd.org/), and [IMF Data](https://www.imf.org/en/Data);
- [ICPSR](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/), [Harvard Dataverse](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/), and discipline repositories;
- official legislation, regulations, court decisions, standards, and agency reports.

A blog or news report may help identify an issue, but cite the underlying study, dataset, legislation, judgment, or official report whenever possible.

### F. Verification and publishing-integrity sites

- [DOI.org](https://doi.org/) — resolve a known DOI.
- [Crossref](https://search.crossref.org/) — verify bibliographic metadata and find DOIs.
- [ORCID](https://orcid.org/) — distinguish researcher identities.
- [Crossmark](https://www.crossref.org/services/crossmark/) — check update/correction/retraction status where publishers use it.
- [Retraction Watch Database](https://retractiondatabase.org/) — investigate retractions.
- [Think. Check. Submit.](https://thinkchecksubmit.org/) — assess journals and publishers.
- [EQUATOR Network](https://www.equator-network.org/) — find reporting guidelines for health research designs.

## 6. How to design and document a literature search

### Step 1: Break the question into concepts

Example topic: **Relationship between social media use and academic performance among university students.**

| Concept | Possible terms |
|---|---|
| Social media | “social media,” social networking, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook |
| Academic performance | “academic performance,” achievement, grades, GPA |
| Population | university students, college students, undergraduates |

### Step 2: Build a Boolean search

```text
("social media" OR "social networking" OR Instagram OR TikTok OR Facebook)
AND
("academic performance" OR achievement OR grades OR GPA)
AND
("university students" OR "college students" OR undergraduate*)
```

Database syntax differs. Check whether the database uses quotation marks, field codes, wildcards, subject headings, proximity operators, or automatic term mapping.

### Step 3: Set eligibility criteria before screening

Example:

- **Include:** empirical studies; university students; English or languages the team can accurately assess; 2016–2026; academic-performance outcome; full text available.
- **Exclude:** school-age populations only; opinion pieces; no academic outcome; duplicate reports; withdrawn/retracted work; inaccessible language without reliable translation.

Do not choose rules only after seeing whether they produce a preferred conclusion.

### Step 4: Keep a search log

Record:

- database/platform;
- exact search string;
- fields searched;
- filters/limits;
- date searched;
- number of records;
- export filename; and
- notes or changes.

### Step 5: Use citation chaining

- **Backward chaining:** inspect the reference list of a key paper.
- **Forward chaining:** use “cited by” to find later work.
- **Author/journal searching:** find related work from major researchers or specialist journals.
- **Similar-article searching:** use related-record functions, then apply normal quality checks.

### Step 6: Stop appropriately

For an ordinary paper, stop when the major concepts, foundational works, recent developments, contrary evidence, and central methods are adequately represented and new searches mostly repeat known findings. For a systematic review, follow the protocol, search all planned sources, update the search if needed, and report the process transparently; “I had enough sources” is not a valid stopping rule.

## 7. Source screening and critical appraisal

### Quick credibility screen

Ask these questions for every central source:

1. **Relevance:** Does it answer the actual question and match the population/context?
2. **Authority:** Who are the authors and issuing organization? Are affiliations and credentials clear?
3. **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed article, preprint, thesis, report, conference paper, editorial, or webpage?
4. **Method:** Is the design appropriate? Are sampling, measures, procedures, and analyses explained?
5. **Bias and limitations:** What selection, measurement, confounding, reporting, or funding biases may exist?
6. **Currency:** Is the source recent enough, and have important later corrections or studies appeared?
7. **Traceability:** Can the data, quotations, DOI, and publication details be verified?
8. **Retraction/correction:** Has the paper been corrected, withdrawn, or retracted?
9. **Conflict of interest:** Who funded the work, and were conflicts disclosed?
10. **Contribution:** Is it primary evidence, synthesis, theory, context, or commentary? Weight it accordingly.

### Use a design-appropriate appraisal tool

- randomized trial: RoB 2 or relevant JBI checklist;
- nonrandomized intervention: ROBINS-I;
- qualitative study: CASP or JBI qualitative checklist;
- mixed-methods study: MMAT;
- systematic review: AMSTAR 2 or ROBIS, where suitable;
- grey literature: AACODS.

A journal’s reputation or an article’s citation count does not replace method appraisal.

## 8. Literature/evidence matrix

Create one row per source. Recommended columns:

1. Record ID
2. Full APA reference
3. DOI/stable URL
4. Publication type and peer-review status
5. Country/context
6. Aim/research question
7. Theory/framework
8. Design and methods
9. Population/sample
10. Variables, measures, or qualitative focus
11. Analysis method
12. Main findings
13. Effect estimate/theme and uncertainty, where applicable
14. Limitations/risk of bias
15. Funding/conflicts
16. Relevant quotation plus page/paragraph number
17. Your paraphrase/interpretation
18. Theme or section in which it will be used
19. Include/exclude decision and reason
20. Verification/retraction status

### Three-note rule

For each important source, write:

- **Source note:** What the author actually reports.
- **Critical note:** Strengths, limits, bias, and relation to other evidence.
- **Use note:** The exact claim or section for which you may cite it.

This prevents a literature review from becoming a collection of summaries.

## 9. From notes to synthesis and argument

### Weak literature review pattern

> Author A said X. Author B said Y. Author C said Z.

### Strong synthesis pattern

> Most cross-sectional studies report an association between X and Y, but longitudinal evidence is less consistent. The difference may reflect measurement and sampling choices: studies using objective measures show smaller effects than those relying on self-report. Evidence is also concentrated in one region, limiting generalization. These limitations support a study using [method] in [understudied context].

### Organize by an analytical principle

Possible structures:

- themes or concepts;
- chronological development;
- theories or models;
- methods and measurement approaches;
- populations or contexts;
- agreement versus controversy;
- causes, effects, and interventions; or
- foundational evidence → current evidence → unresolved gap.

### Paragraph model: claim–evidence–analysis–link

1. **Claim:** State the paragraph’s defensible point.
2. **Evidence:** Present the most relevant source(s), accurately cited.
3. **Analysis:** Compare, qualify, explain, or critique the evidence.
4. **Link:** Show why the point matters to the research question or next paragraph.

Each paragraph should do more than report a source.

### What counts as a defensible research gap?

A gap may be:

- an understudied population, location, period, or outcome;
- inconsistent findings requiring explanation;
- weak or inappropriate previous methods;
- a theory not tested in a relevant context;
- a missing comparison or mechanism;
- a lack of longitudinal, experimental, qualitative, or mixed-methods evidence; or
- evidence that is outdated after a major policy or technological change.

“Few studies exist” is not enough. Explain why the missing evidence matters and how the proposed study addresses it.

## 10. Methodology branches

### A. Quantitative empirical study

Manual tasks include:

- define independent, dependent, control, mediator, and moderator variables where relevant;
- select design: descriptive, correlational, experimental, quasi-experimental, cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, longitudinal, and so on;
- identify population, sampling frame, sampling method, and justified sample size/power;
- select or develop valid, reliable measures;
- predefine data-cleaning rules and statistical analysis;
- check assumptions, missing data, effect sizes, uncertainty/confidence intervals, and sensitivity analyses;
- report results without treating statistical significance as practical importance.

### B. Qualitative empirical study

Manual tasks include:

- define the phenomenon, context, and qualitative approach;
- justify purposive or other sampling and discuss information power/saturation appropriately;
- prepare interview/focus-group/observation protocol;
- obtain consent and protect identities;
- transcribe and code systematically;
- develop themes from evidence, including deviant or negative cases;
- support findings with carefully anonymized excerpts;
- document reflexivity, credibility, dependability, and transferability.

### C. Mixed-methods study

State:

- why one method alone is insufficient;
- whether the design is convergent, explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential, or another form;
- which strand has priority;
- when data are collected and analyzed; and
- exactly how quantitative and qualitative findings are integrated.

### D. Narrative literature review

Use a transparent but suitably flexible search, appraise key evidence, organize it analytically, and explain the search scope. Do not label it “systematic” unless the process meets systematic-review requirements.

### E. Systematic or scoping review

A systematic review requires a protocol, comprehensive reproducible searching, explicit eligibility rules, screening, critical appraisal/risk-of-bias assessment, structured extraction, and transparent synthesis. Use PRISMA 2020 for reporting systematic reviews. Use the appropriate extension, such as PRISMA-ScR, for a scoping review. When feasible, use two independent reviewers for screening/appraisal and a rule for resolving disagreements.

### F. Ethics and data protection

Before collecting data from human participants:

- determine whether institutional ethics/IRB approval is required;
- obtain approval before recruitment or data collection;
- use informed consent and age-appropriate procedures;
- minimize risk and unnecessary sensitive data;
- state access, encryption, retention, sharing, and destruction rules;
- remove direct and indirect identifiers where possible; and
- avoid uploading confidential, proprietary, participant, or unpublished manuscript data to unapproved public tools.

Never invent responses, interviews, measurements, approvals, or statistical outputs.

## 11. Recommended structures

### A. Standard empirical journal paper (IMRaD)

1. Title
2. Abstract and keywords
3. Introduction
   - context and problem;
   - concise literature synthesis/gap;
   - purpose, question/objectives, and hypotheses.
4. Method
   - design;
   - setting/participants/sample;
   - instruments/measures;
   - procedure and ethics;
   - analysis.
5. Results
6. Discussion
   - answer to the question;
   - relation to previous evidence/theory;
   - implications;
   - strengths and limitations;
   - future research.
7. Conclusion
8. References
9. Tables/figures, appendices, and supplementary files as required

### B. Thesis/dissertation chapter model

1. Preliminary pages
2. Chapter 1: Introduction
3. Chapter 2: Literature review/theoretical framework
4. Chapter 3: Methodology
5. Chapter 4: Results/findings
6. Chapter 5: Discussion
7. Chapter 6: Conclusion and recommendations, if the institution uses a separate chapter
8. References
9. Appendices

Follow the institution’s handbook even when it differs from generic APA paper organization.

### C. Stand-alone literature review

1. Introduction and review purpose
2. Search/scope explanation
3. Thematic or analytical sections
4. Critical synthesis and gap
5. Implications/future research
6. Conclusion
7. References

## 12. APA 7th edition quick guide

As of 12 August 2026, the APA Publication Manual, **7th edition**, remains the official APA Style source. Always check the current [APA Style website](https://apastyle.apa.org/) and any institution/journal-specific requirements.

### A. Basic paper formatting

Unless local rules override:

- use 1-inch (2.54 cm) margins;
- double-space the paper, including the reference list;
- use a consistently applied readable font permitted by APA (for example, 12-point Times New Roman, 11-point Calibri, or 11-point Arial);
- left-align text; do not fully justify it;
- indent the first line of ordinary paragraphs by 0.5 inch;
- place the page number at the top right;
- use a separate title page;
- use a running head only when required for the paper type or by the institution/journal;
- use bold APA heading levels consistently; and
- apply a 0.5-inch hanging indent to reference entries.

A student paper title page normally includes the paper title, author, affiliation, course, instructor, due date, and page number. An abstract is included when required.

### B. In-text citations

| Situation | Parenthetical | Narrative |
|---|---|---|
| One author | (Okafor, 2024) | Okafor (2024) |
| Two authors | (Okafor & Smith, 2024) | Okafor and Smith (2024) |
| Three or more authors | (Okafor et al., 2024) | Okafor et al. (2024) |
| Organization, first use with abbreviation | (World Health Organization [WHO], 2024) | World Health Organization (WHO, 2024) |
| Organization, later use | (WHO, 2024) | WHO (2024) |
| Direct quotation | (Okafor, 2024, p. 18) | Okafor (2024) stated “…” (p. 18). |
| Source without pages | (Okafor, 2024, para. 6) | Okafor (2024) stated “…” (para. 6). |
| Multiple sources | (Adeyemi, 2022; Okafor, 2024; Smith, 2023) | Integrate narratively as appropriate. |

For a quotation of **40 words or more**, use a block quotation: begin on a new line, indent the block 0.5 inch, omit quotation marks, and provide the citation. Prefer precise paraphrase and synthesis over excessive quotation.

### C. Reference-list principles

- Include every retrievable source cited in the text, and cite every reference-list entry in the text.
- Alphabetize by the first author’s surname or organizational author.
- Use initials for given names.
- Use sentence case for article, book, report, chapter, and webpage titles.
- Italicize journal title and volume number; place issue number in parentheses without italics.
- Include a DOI whenever one exists, in the format `https://doi.org/...`.
- If an online work has both a DOI and a URL, use the DOI only.
- For most articles retrieved from ordinary academic databases without a DOI, do not add the database name or database URL.
- Do not place a period after a DOI or URL.
- Use retrieval dates only for content designed to change over time when appropriate.

### D. Common reference templates

#### Journal article with DOI

```text
Author, A. A., & Author, B. B. (Year). Title of article: Subtitle. Journal Title, 12(3), 123–145. https://doi.org/10.xxxx/xxxxx
```

#### Book

```text
Author, A. A. (Year). Title of book: Subtitle (2nd ed.). Publisher. https://doi.org/xxxxx
```

#### Chapter in an edited book

```text
Author, A. A. (Year). Title of chapter. In E. E. Editor & F. F. Editor (Eds.), Title of book (pp. 25–48). Publisher. https://doi.org/xxxxx
```

#### Webpage

```text
Author or Organization. (Year, Month Day). Title of webpage. Site Name. URL
```

Omit the site name when it is the same as the author. Use `(n.d.)` when no date is available.

#### Government or organizational report

```text
Organization Name. (Year). Title of report (Report No. 123, if applicable). Publisher if different from author. URL
```

#### Thesis or dissertation from a database/repository

```text
Author, A. A. (Year). Title of dissertation [Doctoral dissertation, University Name]. Repository or Database. URL
```

#### Dataset

```text
Author, A. A. (Year). Title of dataset (Version) [Data set]. Repository. https://doi.org/xxxxx
```

### E. Tables and figures

- Number tables and figures in the order mentioned.
- Give each a concise italicized title.
- Refer to every table/figure in the text.
- Explain abbreviations and necessary source/permission details in notes.
- Cite adapted or reproduced material and obtain permission where required.
- Do not repeat the entire table in prose; explain its most important pattern.

### F. APA citation audit

Before submission:

1. Match every in-text citation to one reference entry.
2. Match every reference entry to at least one in-text citation, except limited items treated differently by APA.
3. Open every DOI/URL.
4. Verify author order, year, title, journal/book, volume, issue, pages, and DOI against the original/publisher record.
5. Check quotations word for word and confirm the page/paragraph locator.
6. Check capitalization, italics, punctuation, alphabetical order, and hanging indents.

Do not trust a citation generator without checking its output.

## 13. Other styles you may encounter

Use the style named in the brief or journal guide:

- **MLA:** common in language and literature;
- **Chicago/Turabian:** common in history and some humanities, using notes-bibliography or author-date;
- **Harvard:** author-date family with institution-specific variations;
- **Vancouver:** numbered citations, common in medicine;
- **IEEE:** numbered bracketed citations, common in engineering/computing;
- **AMA:** common in medicine and health sciences.

Never convert to APA merely because it is familiar if the required style is different.

## 14. Drafting workflow

### Draft in a controlled order

A practical order is:

1. method/protocol;
2. tables, figures, and results or synthesis outputs;
3. results/findings;
4. literature/background;
5. discussion;
6. introduction;
7. conclusion and recommendations;
8. abstract;
9. title and keywords.

This order reduces the risk that the introduction promises a study different from the one actually completed.

### Use placeholders safely

Acceptable temporary markers:

```text
[ADD VERIFIED SOURCE]
[CHECK NUMBER AGAINST TABLE 2]
[CONFIRM ETHICS APPROVAL NUMBER]
[EXPLAIN CONTRADICTORY RESULT]
```

Search and resolve every marker before submission. Never fill a gap with a guessed citation, statistic, quotation, or approval number.

### Keep results and discussion distinct

- **Results/findings:** What the analysis found.
- **Discussion:** What the findings mean, how they relate to theory/previous evidence, and what their limits and implications are.

## 15. Plagiarism, paraphrasing, authorship, and AI

### Avoiding plagiarism

- cite ideas as well as exact words;
- put exact borrowed language in quotation marks or a block quote and add a locator;
- paraphrase from understanding, not by replacing a few words with synonyms;
- compare a paraphrase with the source to ensure both originality and accuracy;
- cite your own earlier work when reusing it, subject to publisher/institution rules;
- obtain permission for copyrighted material when required; and
- interpret similarity reports—matching text is not automatically plagiarism, and a low score does not prove integrity.

### Responsible AI use

Follow the institution, funder, ethics board, and target journal’s current policy. At minimum:

- do not list an AI tool as an author;
- disclose tool use when required, including what was used and for what purpose;
- personally verify every fact, citation, calculation, quotation, and interpretation;
- do not upload confidential participant data, private peer-review material, unpublished manuscripts, exam material, or proprietary data to unapproved tools;
- do not use AI to fabricate data, respondents, images presented as genuine observations, analysis, ethics approvals, or references; and
- retain human responsibility for the final work.

The Committee on Publication Ethics states that AI tools cannot satisfy authorship responsibilities and that authors remain responsible for AI-assisted content.

## 16. Quality-control sequence

Do not attempt one giant proofreading pass. Use separate passes:

### Pass 1: Requirements

- correct task, title, document type, word count, deadline, style, structure, and file type;
- every rubric item addressed;
- target-journal or institutional instructions followed.

### Pass 2: Alignment and argument

- problem, question, objectives, method, results, and conclusion agree;
- the thesis/main contribution is explicit;
- headings follow a logical sequence;
- each paragraph has a clear function;
- counterevidence and alternative explanations are considered.

### Pass 3: Evidence

- every factual or literature-based claim has adequate support;
- each citation genuinely supports the sentence;
- seminal, recent, and contrary evidence are represented where relevant;
- no retracted work is relied on unknowingly;
- primary sources are used where available.

### Pass 4: Method and data

- procedures are sufficiently transparent;
- sample, variables/themes, instruments, ethics, and analysis are explained;
- tables and text contain the same numbers;
- effect directions, units, labels, totals, percentages, uncertainty, and rounding are correct;
- conclusions do not exceed the design’s causal or generalizable limits.

### Pass 5: Citations and APA

- complete two-way citation/reference match;
- DOI/URL and metadata verification;
- quotation locator check;
- title page, headings, spacing, tables, figures, and references formatted correctly.

### Pass 6: Language and accessibility

- concise sentences and precise verbs;
- consistent terminology, tense, person, abbreviations, and spelling variant;
- bias-free, respectful language;
- accessible table headings, figure labels, and alt text where required;
- no tracked changes, comments, highlights, or unresolved placeholders in the final file.

### Pass 7: Final-file test

Open the exact submitted files on another device or PDF viewer. Check page breaks, fonts, equations, symbols, figure resolution, hyperlinks, anonymization, file names, and supplementary files.

## 17. Project management system

### Recommended folder structure

```text
01_Brief_and_Guidelines/
02_Protocol_and_Ethics/
03_Search_Exports/
04_PDF_Library/
05_Screening_and_Appraisal/
06_Evidence_Matrix/
07_Data_Raw_READONLY/
08_Data_Clean_and_Code/
09_Analysis_Outputs/
10_Drafts/
11_Tables_Figures/
12_Submission/
13_Archive/
```

Never overwrite raw data. Keep it read-only and work on a documented copy.

### File naming

```text
Project_ShortTitle_Document_v01_2026-08-12.docx
Project_ShortTitle_Document_v02_SUPERVISOR-COMMENTS_2026-08-18.docx
Project_ShortTitle_FINAL-SUBMITTED_2026-09-01.pdf
```

Do not use confusing names such as `final2_realfinal_latest.docx`.

### Reference manager minimum workflow

1. Create a collection for the project.
2. Import metadata from the database or DOI.
3. Attach the lawful PDF or stable link.
4. Correct title case, authors, date, journal, volume, issue, pages, and DOI.
5. Add tags/notes for theme and screening status.
6. Back up or sync the library appropriately.
7. Insert citations with the word-processor plugin.
8. Refresh the bibliography and then run a manual audit.

[Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/) is a strong free option; EndNote and Mendeley are alternatives. Avoid manually retyping every reference if a manager is available, but still verify the final output.

## 20. Definition of done

The work is complete only when:

- the final document answers the stated research question;
- the evidence and method can support the conclusions;
- the literature search and source selection are appropriate to the paper type;
- central sources were read, appraised, and accurately represented;
- all data, quotations, and citations are verifiable;
- limitations and contrary evidence are acknowledged;
- authorship, ethics, funding, conflicts, AI use, and permissions are disclosed as required;
- the document follows the required style and submission instructions;
- all tables, figures, appendices, links, and supplementary files work; and
- the project files are safely archived with clear version history.
